A field guide to the rhythms

Hz means cycles per second. Each miniature below shows exactly one second at the same amplitude.

13–30 Hz

Beta β

Fast activity often present during wakefulness. A beta rhythm is not a direct meter of attention or stress.

8–13 Hz

Alpha α

Often prominent over the back of the head in relaxed adults with eyes closed; usually diminishes when the eyes open.

4–8 Hz

Theta θ

Slower activity that becomes more prominent with drowsiness and light sleep. Its meaning depends on age and context.

~0.5–4 Hz

Delta δ

Slow waves prominent in deep non-REM sleep. Coordinated active and silent periods can accompany slow-wave activity.

Frequency boundaries vary slightly between conventions. Real EEG mixes rhythms rather than switching between pure sine waves. The miniature amplitudes are equal for comparison, not typical measured voltages.

Sleep is more than slowing down.

Light non-REM sleep includes distinctive features such as sleep spindles and K-complexes. Deep non-REM sleep has prominent slow waves. REM sleep can look more like wakefulness on EEG. This simplified demonstration leaves out those features and REM.

What clinicians actually read

Frequency is only one clue. Clinicians also examine amplitude (usually in microvolts), waveform shape, location, symmetry, and changes over time. Sleep staging combines EEG with eye movements and muscle activity. A band alone cannot diagnose a condition or reveal a thought.

Read more: American Epilepsy Society: the normal EEG · NIH: sleep phases and stages. Educational illustration, not a clinical measurement.
